How this is calculated

  • Orbits. Circular, 650 km, with the slow turning of each orbit plane caused by Earth's equatorial bulge (J2). It is the same model as the project scripts (coverage_check.py, constellation_count.py), so the 94-night figures should match them.
  • Look. The satellite passes within half a swath of the place (467.5 km for the 935 km swath) between 19:00 and 05:00 local solar time. The pre-dawn gap is 03:00 to 06:00. Passes then are counted separately, since 05:00 to 06:00 falls outside the night used for looks.
  • Orbit choice. The peat that burns most lies between about 0.5°N and 3.5°S, so a 4° orbit keeps both satellites over it and gives every province a 03:00–06:00 look each night. Indonesia's LAPAN-A2 already flies a 6° orbit of this kind. The 935 km swath comes from NASA's SBG-TIR design.
  • Province points. Approximate centres of burning peat, not exact fire locations.
  • Clouds. Not on the map. The clear-look chance treats every look as independent, so it is an upper bound.
  • Times. Local solar time at each site, which differs from clock time (WIB, WITA, WIT) by up to about 35 minutes. Site positions are approximate centres.
  • NISAR. A radar, so it cannot see heat. In this NISAR-like orbit it passes each site every few days at about 06:00 or 18:00, never between 19:00 and 05:00.
  • Not modelled. Pixel growth at the swath edge, haze, and the real NISAR 12-day repeat track.
